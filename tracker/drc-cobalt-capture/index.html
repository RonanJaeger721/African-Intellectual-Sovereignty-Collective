Who Captured the Cobalt?

The Democratic Republic of Congo supplies the largest share of the world's mined cobalt. The terms on which it leaves are negotiated under sharply unequal access to information.

Corruption Investigation Desk3 min read / October edition
In this article

There is a number that should unsettle anyone who follows African political economy. The DRC supplied around three-quarters of the world's mined cobalt in 2024. Endowment, however, is not the same as domestic value capture. The distance between those two questions is where this story lives. [4]

The asset and the asymmetry

Cobalt is not an ordinary commodity. It remains important to nickel-based battery chemistries, but it is not required by every EV battery. Lithium iron phosphate batteries contain no cobalt and are already commercially widespread. The energy transition still runs through Congo, but the changing chemistry of demand matters to its bargaining position. [1]

Endowment, however, is not revenue, and in Congo the gap is shaped by contracts. The 2008 Sicomines mining-for-infrastructure agreement is an instructive case. Its original infrastructure commitment was USD 3 billion; the mining investment was a separate component of the broader arrangement. EITI disclosures informed scrutiny and renegotiation, and in 2024 the infrastructure envelope was increased to USD 7 billion. The 2018 Mining Code raised strategic-mineral royalties and introduced a super-profits tax. Whether the resulting revenue matches the legal promise remains an enforcement question, not a finding established by the draft alone. [2]

The mechanism does not depend on illegality. It runs on three asymmetries.

Information. When negotiators enter mining talks without the commodity price models, reserve certifications or offtake arithmetic that counterparties hold, the bargain starts on unequal terms. A contract agreed under that kind of capacity gap can be formally legitimate and still deliver poor public value for decades. Establishing that this happened in a particular negotiation requires its own documentary record.

Time. Renegotiation windows open rarely: a price spike, a change of government, an assertive parliament. Each one that closes without reform locks in the old terms for the life of the concession.

Enforcement. Even the improved code of 2018 depends on a tax authority, a customs service and a courts system with the capacity and the protection to apply it. The distance between the law and collection is where the value disappears.

The shift nobody priced

CMOC is the largest cobalt producer, with output from both Tenke Fungurume and Kisanfu. Its 2024 results report 114,165 tonnes of cobalt. The growth cannot be attributed to Tenke Fungurume alone. The centre of gravity of an important part of the battery supply chain sits in the DRC, and the terms of access increasingly involve companies with substantial state connections. [3]

This is not a story with a simple villain. Congolese institutions have secured better terms through renegotiation, as the 2024 Sicomines agreement illustrates. Artisanal mining also raises serious labour, safety and due-diligence questions. Those questions need dated labour and due-diligence evidence; industrial production totals cannot answer them. [2]

Our analysis, stated plainly: Congo has moved from poorly scrutinised bargains toward more visibly contested terms. The phrase 'contested capture' is our political-economy interpretation, not a finding of criminal wrongdoing. Public disclosure and institutional resistance have changed terms; measuring the final public benefit still requires contract, payment and delivery records.

Fact box

  • Global share: around three-quarters of mined cobalt in 2024; the year and production measure matter
  • Sicomines: original infrastructure commitment of USD 3bn, increased to USD 7bn in the 2024 renegotiation
  • Battery demand: cobalt is used in nickel-based chemistries; commercial LFP batteries use none
  • Watch item: contract disclosure, enforcement and public benefit under revised terms
