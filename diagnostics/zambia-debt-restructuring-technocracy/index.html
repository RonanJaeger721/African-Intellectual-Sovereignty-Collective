The Debt Decade: What Zambia's Default Actually Taught the Continent

Lusaka's default and recovery is the most instructive economic governance story in Africa this decade, and its lessons are being forgotten almost as fast as they were earned.

Governance Analysis Desk3 min read / October edition
In this article

In November 2020, Zambia became the first African country to default on its sovereign debt in the pandemic era. Unwinding the consequences took years.

The case matters because it is the most complete test the continent has run of what happens between a government borrowing past its limits and a government coming back from the brink. That middle passage, default to recovery, is where the lessons live. Most coverage stops at the headline. The accounting is worth doing properly.

What actually broke

Zambia's crisis was not simply a coronavirus story, whatever the timing suggested. The IMF had already assessed its debt as unsustainable in 2019. The borrowing itself was scattered rather than hidden: eurobonds, bilateral infrastructure finance and domestic obligations. Different debt measures cover different liabilities; they should not be collapsed into a single unexplained growth figure. [1]

The failure that mattered most was the architecture of accountability. A consolidated, inspectable debt register is a negotiating asset as well as a public safeguard. A state that cannot explain what it owes, to whom and on what terms weakens its own bargaining position. That is the institutional diagnosis here, not a claim to have observed confidential negotiating sessions.

The repair, and its price

The government elected in August 2021 made debt restructuring a defining project. An IMF programme was approved in 2022; an agreement with official creditors under the G20 Common Framework followed in 2023, and a Eurobond restructuring followed in 2024. China and France co-chaired the official creditor committee. These were major milestones, not proof that every debt problem had closed. Zambia was not the first African Common Framework agreement: Chad reached an agreement in 2022. [1] [2] [3]

Publishing the creditor register can hurt at home and help at the table. Disclosure exposes uncomfortable obligations, but believable books give creditors and citizens a common starting point. The argument for transparency is strongest when it survives the crisis that made it politically necessary.

The Common Framework can deliver agreements, but slowly, and slowness carries costs. Zambia spent years in restructuring. The lesson for the next debtor is preparation: early engagement, usable records and creditor coordination matter. Comparisons between debtors need a consistent measure of delay and cost.

Restructuring without revenue reform is a stay of execution. Zambia's finances remain exposed to copper, a commodity capable of delivering windfalls to treasuries that spend them. The deeper question is whether a resource-dependent state can institutionalise restraint through fiscal rules, credible budgeting and transparent revenue management. The institutional task is to make restraint permanent rather than dependent on the next crisis.

The diagnostic

Our assessment is that Zambia responded more convincingly to crisis than its earlier safeguards prevented it. Acknowledging failure, negotiating and implementing reform are different capabilities from preventing unsustainable borrowing. This is a qualitative editorial diagnosis, not a validated comparative country score.

A state can be excellent at managing a disaster it should never have had. The prescription follows from the finding: the borrowing-transparency architecture Zambia adopted under duress needs to be made permanent, legislated, and insulated from the political cycle that made it necessary.

Fact box

  • Default: November 2020, the first African pandemic-era sovereign default
  • Debt sustainability: assessed as unsustainable by the IMF in 2019
  • Milestones: IMF programme in 2022, official-creditor agreement in 2023, Eurobond restructuring in 2024
  • Watch item: copper dependence, fiscal rules and transparent borrowing records
